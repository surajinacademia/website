# Literature survey editorial notes

These notes record meaning-sensitive claims from the original Notion export. The website
fragment preserves their intent so that a copyedit does not silently change the author's
argument. They should be reviewed before public release.

## Claims to review

- **“There are no formal consequences” for inaccuracies or misleading information.** Scientific
  publishing is not a legal proceeding, but journals and institutions can issue corrections,
  retractions, sanctions, or findings of misconduct. A more precise version could focus on the
  reader's responsibility to assess whether a paper supports its claims.
- **Authors “often” cite work without fully comprehending it, described as a hallmark of the
  publishing industry.** This is a broad empirical claim without supporting evidence in the source.
  The intended lesson can be stated more defensibly: citations may support a narrow point, repeat an
  indirect claim, or omit important context, so important claims should be traced to their original
  evidence.
- **The author's social status and reputation are “irrelevant.”** Prestige cannot substitute for
  evaluating methods and evidence, but “irrelevant” is absolute. Reputation and venue can affect
  which work receives attention while remaining unreliable proxies for the truth of a claim.
- **“Witnesses pay to have their statements published.”** Publication models vary. Some journals
  charge article-processing or page fees, many do not, and the author may not personally pay. The
  intended point—that publication in a prestigious venue does not guarantee reliability—does not
  require this claim.
- **Notero “automatically” syncs to Notion.** This depends on the integration's current setup and
  behavior. Verify it against current Notero documentation before presenting it as a universal step.
- **Terrence Malick and Scorsese first write scripts before shooting.** The filmmaking analogy is
  central to the author's voice, but the named-director claim needs a source or a version that does
  not depend on a universal statement about their process.
- **“Most presentations are character-driven.”** “Character-driven” and “plot-driven” are not
  defined for scientific presentations here, and “most” is unsupported. This could remain a personal
  observation or become a question that introduces the distinction.
